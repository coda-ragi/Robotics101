### Introduction

In the dynamic field of robotics, understanding a robot's physical capabilities and performance metrics is paramount for effective design, selection, and task execution. Just as an architect must understand the structural limits of a building, a roboticist must grasp the operational boundaries of a manipulator. This lecture delves into two critical aspects: the robot's workspace, which defines its reachable physical space, and its performance characteristics, including resolution, accuracy, and repeatability, which dictate its precision and consistency.

The ability to visualize and analyze a robot's workspace, especially for complex multi-joint systems, is fundamental to determining if a robot can even perform a given task. Beyond reach, the precision with which a robot can execute movements and return to specific points is crucial for applications ranging from delicate assembly to robust manufacturing. This session will clarify these concepts, providing a foundational understanding necessary for anyone involved in specifying, programming, or deploying robotic systems.

### Core Concepts

| Term | Definition | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`Workspace`** | The total volume of space that the end-effector of a robot can reach. | Essential for determining if a robot can physically perform a given task or reach all necessary points. |
| **`Programming Resolution`** | The smallest allowable position increment that can be specified in a robot's computer program. | Defines the granularity of movement commands, impacting the precision of programmed paths. |
| **`Basic Resolution Unit (BRU)`** | A standard unit used to express programming resolution, typically 0.01 inch or 0.1 degree for rotary motion. | Provides a quantifiable measure for the smallest programmable step, crucial for robot specification. |
| **`Control Resolution`** | The smallest position increment that a robot's feedback control system can detect and respond to. | Dictates the precision of the robot's actual movement based on sensor feedback, often limited by encoder resolution. |
| **`Optical Encoder`** | A feedback device used in robots to measure angular displacement by detecting light passing through patterned discs. | A common sensor for determining joint positions, directly influencing the control resolution of rotary joints. |
| **`Accuracy`** | The precision with which a robot can reach a *computed* target point in space. | Measures how close the robot's actual position is to the theoretically calculated position, indicating systematic errors. |
| **`Repeatability`** | The ability of a robot to consistently return to a *taught* point over multiple attempts. | Measures the consistency of robot performance, crucial for tasks requiring high precision and consistency in repetitive operations. |

### Detailed Analysis

#### 1. Robot Workspace Analysis

The workspace of a robot is the total volume of space its end-effector can reach. This volume varies significantly depending on the robot's kinematic structure, specifically its coordinate system and joint types. For complex manipulators, visualizing the 3D workspace can be challenging, often requiring the use of multiple 2D views like elevation and plan views.

* **Cartesian Coordinate Robot**:
* Characterized by three linear joints.
* Its workspace is a simple cuboid.
* Visualizing this is straightforward; an elevation view shows a rectangle (horizontal and vertical strokes), and a plan view shows another rectangle (horizontal and transverse strokes).

* **Cylindrical Coordinate Robot**:
* Typically features two linear joints and one rotary (twisting) joint.
* The workspace is a cylindrical annular space.
* The rotary joint allows 360-degree rotation around a fixed base, creating a circular footprint in the plan view, bounded by maximum and minimum horizontal reaches.
* The linear joints define the vertical reach and the radial extent.

* **Spherical Coordinate Robot**:
* Comprises one linear joint, one revolute joint, and one twisting joint.
* The workspace is more complex than cylindrical, often resembling a partial sphere or a section of a sphere.
* The linear joint defines the radial reach, while the revolute and twisting joints allow for angular movement.
* To imagine its 3D workspace, one must visualize rotating the elevation view (showing maximum/minimum horizontal and vertical reach) through the angle provided by the twisting joint (e.g., 330 degrees).

* **Revolute Coordinate Robot (Articulated Robot)**:
* Characterized by multiple rotary joints (e.g., three rotary joints).
* Its workspace is the most difficult to visualize due to the intersection of partial spheres generated by the rotary movements.
* A simplified sketch helps in understanding:
* Consider a base twisting joint (e.g., 0 to 330 degrees).
* A second revolute joint (e.g., 0 to 90 degrees).
* A third revolute joint (e.g., -90 to +90 degrees).
* The combination of these joint limits defines a complex, often irregular, volume.
* The elevation view of such a robot, showing its folded-back and stretched conditions, must be rotated by the base twisting joint's range to conceptualize the full 3D workspace.
* For robots with many degrees of freedom, like a PUMA robot with six rotary joints, visualizing the workspace becomes extremely challenging, necessitating advanced computational tools.
* Workspace analysis is crucial before assigning any task to a manipulator to ensure the end-effector can reach all required points.

#### 2. Robot Performance Metrics: Resolution, Accuracy, and Repeatability

These three terms are fundamental for specifying and evaluating robot performance, though they are distinct in their meaning.

* **Resolution**:
* Refers to the smallest increment of position or angle a robot can measure or move.
* **Programming Resolution**: This is the smallest position increment allowed in the robot's control software. It is often expressed in Basic Resolution Units (BRU), where 1 BRU might be 0.01 inch or 0.001 millimeter for linear motion, and 0.1 degree for rotary motion. This defines the "least count" of the robot's programmable movement.
* **Control Resolution**: This is determined by the feedback devices used in the robot's control system, such as optical encoders. For example, if an optical encoder provides 1000 electrical pulses for a 360-degree rotation, its control resolution is 0.36 degrees per pulse. The robot cannot detect or respond to movements smaller than this increment.

* **Accuracy**:
* Defined as the precision with which a robot can reach a *computed* point.
* Consider a 2-degrees-of-freedom serial manipulator with link lengths L1 and L2, and joint angles theta1 and theta2. The theoretical end-effector position (x, y) can be calculated using forward kinematics:
* x = L1 cos(theta1) + L2 cos(theta1 + theta2)
* y = L1 sin(theta1) + L2 sin(theta1 + theta2)
* When the robot attempts to reach this calculated (x, y) point, there might be a small deviation due to mechanical imperfections, calibration errors, or environmental factors.
* Accuracy measures this deviation between the computed target point and the actual point reached by the robot. It can be positive or negative and is typically expressed in units like millimeters.

* **Repeatability**:
* Refers to the robot's ability to consistently return to a *taught* point over multiple attempts.
* Unlike accuracy, repeatability does not compare the robot's position to a theoretical calculation but rather to a previously recorded or "taught" position.
* If a robot is taught to reach a specific point (e.g., point A) and then commanded to return to that point multiple times (e.g., 20 times), it will likely exhibit a small scatter of positions around the original taught point.
* Repeatability quantifies this scatter or deviation from the taught point. A robot with high repeatability will consistently return to very close to the same spot, even if that spot is slightly off from its theoretically accurate position.
* Repeatability is often more critical than accuracy in many industrial applications where consistent execution of a task is prioritized over absolute positional precision.

When purchasing or designing a robot, it is essential to specify the required resolution, accuracy, and repeatability based on the application's demands, as these metrics directly impact the robot's suitability for a given task.

### Key Takeaways
- [ ] The workspace defines the physical reach of a robot's end-effector and is crucial for task feasibility.
- [ ] Robot workspaces vary significantly based on their coordinate system (Cartesian, Cylindrical, Spherical, Revolute).
- [ ] Visualizing complex 3D workspaces often requires using 2D elevation and plan views.
- [ ] Revolute coordinate robots have the most complex workspaces due to multiple rotary joints creating intersecting partial spheres.
- [ ] Resolution is the smallest increment a robot can measure or move, categorized into programming and control resolution.
- [ ] Programming resolution is defined by Basic Resolution Units (BRU) in the control software.
- [ ] Control resolution is determined by the precision of feedback devices like optical encoders.
- [ ] Accuracy measures how close a robot gets to a *computed* target point.
- [ ] Repeatability measures how consistently a robot returns to a *taught* point over multiple attempts.
- [ ] Understanding and specifying resolution, accuracy, and repeatability are vital for selecting and deploying robots for specific tasks.

### Conclusion

The exploration of robot workspaces and performance metrics provides a foundational understanding for anyone engaging with robotic systems. From the simple cuboid of a Cartesian robot to the intricate, multi-spherical intersections of an articulated manipulator, the concept of workspace dictates the fundamental "can it reach?" question. This geometric understanding, often aided by 2D projections, is the first step in determining a robot's suitability for a given operational environment.

Beyond mere reach, the precision and consistency of a robot's movements are quantified by its resolution, accuracy, and repeatability. These distinct yet interconnected metrics are not just theoretical constructs; they are critical specifications that directly influence a robot's ability to perform tasks like delicate assembly, precise welding, or consistent pick-and-place operations. By grasping these concepts, engineers and designers can make informed decisions, ensuring that the chosen robotic solution not only fits within the physical constraints of a task but also meets the required levels of precision and reliability, thereby optimizing performance and efficiency in real-world applications.
